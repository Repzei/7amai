### 1. Anthropic reportedly spends $3.4B on its own gas generators {#anthropic-gas-generators}
<p class="item-meta"><span class="signal signal-worth">Worth knowing</span><span class="track">General</span><span class="track">Build</span><span class="track">1 min</span></p>
<div class="number"><b>$3.4B</b><span>for gas generators to power Anthropic's data centers, the FT reports</span></div>

<p class="short"><span>The short version</span>Anthropic has bought gas generators worth about $3.4 billion to power its data centers, the Financial Times reports. AI labs now buy their own power, not only chips.</p>

Every answer from Claude runs on a chip in a data center, and every chip needs electricity. So the limit on AI is not only chips. It is also power.

According to the Financial Times, Anthropic agreed in late September to buy heavy-duty gas generators from OnePWR Solutions in Texas. The deal is worth about $3.4 billion and covers 1.7 gigawatts.

That fits a promise Anthropic made in February: to bring new power online for its data centers, so local households do not pay more. It also said US AI will need at least 50 gigawatts within a few years.

The catch: this is a newspaper report. Anthropic and OnePWR did not comment, and Reuters could not confirm it.

<div class="why"><b>Why it matters</b><p>It is like a busy restaurant that buys its own generator, because the city cannot lay a bigger cable fast enough. How fast Claude can grow now depends on power as much as on chips.</p></div>

<p class="item-meta"><span class="signal signal-worth">Worth knowing</span><span class="track">General</span><span class="track">1 min</span></p>

<p class="short"><span>The short version</span>Nvidia's board has started an independent review after reports that CEO Jensen Huang was the target of a Chinese spy. The board says it sees no sign that secrets leaked.</p>

Nvidia's chips are at the center of the AI race between the US and China. That makes its CEO a natural target.

On Sunday, the board said independent directors, advised by outside lawyers, will look into the reports. Huang supports the review. In a message to staff, he admitted an affair and apologized. He did not name the woman.

TechCrunch names her as Alice Huang, a former executive at the Singapore chip firm Megaspeed. A memo shared among US policymakers claimed that China's Ministry of State Security recruited her to spy on him.

The catch: the spy claim comes from a memo that has not been published. The board says it has "no reason to believe" that national security or company secrets were compromised.

<div class="why"><b>Why it matters</b><p>When one company sits at the center of a race between two countries, its CEO's private life becomes a security question. And US rules decide where Nvidia may sell its chips.</p></div>

### 3. Microsoft will run Claude in Copilot inside the EU by the end of 2026 {#claude-copilot-eu}
<p class="item-meta"><span class="signal signal-worth">Worth knowing</span><span class="track">Europe</span><span class="track">General</span><span class="track">1½ min</span></p>

<p class="short"><span>The short version</span>European firms that use Copilot will get Claude processed inside the EU by the end of 2026, Microsoft says. Until an admin turns it on, Claude stays off.</p>

Many European firms want their data to stay inside the EU. Microsoft promises that for Microsoft 365 through its "EU Data Boundary". Claude was not part of that promise, so firms had to choose: Claude, or data processing in Europe.

Microsoft says that choice will go away. By the end of 2026, firms in the EU and EFTA should be able to use Claude across Copilot. It will run in the region where their data already lives.

For EU firms, Claude is off by default, and an admin can turn it on. Firms that already use it do not have to do anything.

The catch: there is no exact date yet, only "by the end of 2026".

<div class="why"><b>Why it matters</b><p>It is like a bank opening a branch in your own country: same service, but your money no longer crosses a border. For a European firm, that can turn a "no" from its lawyers into a "yes".</p></div>

<p class="todo"><b>Do this:</b> If your firm uses Copilot, ask your admin if Claude should be switched on. It can be done now.</p>

### 4. Apple wants Siri to act on its own as early as next year, Bloomberg reports {#siri-agent-2027}
<p class="item-meta"><span class="signal signal-quick">Quick hit</span><span class="track">General</span><span class="track">Build</span><span class="track">1½ min</span></p>

<p class="short"><span>The short version</span>Apple wants to make Siri more like an agent that acts for you, perhaps as early as 2027, Bloomberg's Mark Gurman reports. Today's Siri still waits to be told what to do.</p>

The new personal Siri arrived with iOS 27. It knows more about you, but it still cannot take action on your behalf on its own. Many AI firms are working on exactly that.

According to Gurman's Power On newsletter, Apple wants Siri to become "more like an agent", and it could happen next year. Apple already has App Intents, a way for apps to tell Siri what they can do. That could let Siri act in apps without clicking around the screen like a person, which costs more.

Apple has also given engineers more time to try ideas for iOS 28: six weeks of coding and exploring, where a stage usually lasts four.

The catch: this is a report about plans, and Apple has said nothing. "As early as" next year can also mean later.

### Microsoft's small computer-use agent now runs in llama.cpp {#mage-vl-cowork-local}
<p class="item-meta"><span class="signal signal-worth">Worth knowing</span><span class="track">Build</span><span class="track">General</span><span class="track">Europe</span><span class="track">1½ min</span></p>
<div class="number"><b>66.0%</b><span>of real desktop tasks solved, against 72.4% for humans</span></div>

<p class="short"><span>The short version</span>Mage-VL-Cowork, a small Microsoft model that can use a computer for you, now runs in llama.cpp. So it can work on your own PC, without sending your screen to the cloud.</p>

An AI agent that uses your computer has to see your screen. With a cloud agent, screenshots of your desktop go to someone else's server.

Mage-VL-Cowork is small enough for a normal PC. It reads screenshots, clicks and types, and runs terminal commands when that is quicker. On OSWorld-Verified, a test of real desktop tasks, it solves 66.0%. Humans reach 72.4%.

On Saturday, llama.cpp added support, and ready-made versions exist for Ollama and LM Studio too. The Apache 2.0 license lets firms use it freely.

The catch: these are Microsoft's own results. An agent that runs commands can also delete things, so the model card says to keep it in an isolated container.

<div class="why"><b>Why it matters</b><p>It is like a helper who works in your office instead of taking your files home. For anyone who may not send their screen out of the building, a local agent may be the only option.</p></div>

### AI agents waste about 18% of what they write, a study finds {#agent-token-waste}
<p class="item-meta"><span class="signal signal-worth">Worth knowing</span><span class="track">Build</span><span class="track">General</span><span class="track">1½ min</span></p>
<div class="number"><b>18%</b><span>of AI agents' output tokens are waste, a study finds</span></div>

<p class="short"><span>The short version</span>Researchers measured how much of an AI agent's output does nothing useful. About 18% is waste, and the tool around the model matters more than the model.</p>

When you pay for an AI agent, you pay for every token (a small piece of text) it writes, also when it repeats itself.

A team led by Huimin Zeng let a judge model mark text that could be removed. Then they removed it and ran the task again, to check that the result held. They compared five agent tools, called harnesses, on the same model.

About 18% of the output was waste, mostly detours that turned out not to be needed. Agents also restated their plan and read the same file again. In more than 45% of tasks, one agent's waste alone was bigger than another agent's whole output.

The catch: the cuts were tested on only two benchmarks. There, removing 41-46% of the reasoning left results about the same.

<div class="why"><b>Why it matters</b><p>It is like two taxi drivers with the same car, where one takes detours and you pay the meter. The waste varied more between tools and tasks than between models, so the tool you pick can change your bill more than the model.</p></div>

Source: [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07238) · <span class="tag tag-official">Official</span>

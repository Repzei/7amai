The Riemann hypothesis is a guess about how the prime numbers are spread out. Primes are the numbers like 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11 that only divide by 1 and themselves.

Primes look random when you list them. But they thin out at a rate mathematicians can predict quite well. The open question is how far the real count can drift from that prediction.

In 1859, Bernhard Riemann linked that question to a special function, now called the zeta function. He guessed where its "interesting" zeros lie: all on one straight line, where the real part is 1/2. If he was right, the primes stray from the prediction only by a small, known amount.

> **Think of it like this:** Picture a long road with houses placed at what looks like random spots. You can predict roughly how many houses there are per kilometer. The Riemann hypothesis says the real count never strays far from that guess, anywhere on the road.

Computers have checked the first ten trillion zeros, and all of them sit on the line. But checking many cases is not a proof, because the zeros go on forever.

It is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, announced by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000. A stronger version, the Generalized Riemann hypothesis, makes the same claim for a whole family of similar functions.

**Why it matters to you:** A proof would shed light on how the primes are spread out. It would also settle a question that has shaped math for over 160 years. If a claimed proof holds, it also shows how far AI and computer-checked proofs have come.

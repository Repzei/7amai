A deepfake is a photo, video or sound clip that an AI has changed or made from scratch. In it, a real person seems to say or do something they never did. Good ones look and sound real.

> **Think of it like this:** a forger who has studied thousands of photos of your face. Ask for a new picture, and the forger paints you somewhere you have never been, in a pose you never struck.

It works because AI models learn patterns from many examples. Trained on many faces, a model learns how a face looks from every angle. It can then put one person's face on another body, change what a mouth says, or make a voice read new words.

The US Government Accountability Office names two classic methods. One squeezes a face into a simple description and rebuilds it, which lets you swap faces. The other sets two models against each other. One makes fakes and the other tries to catch them, so the fakes improve each round. Today's image models can do the same from a single photo and a written request.

Anyone with a home computer can make one, the GAO says. And most deepfakes online are sexual images made without consent, which hit women hardest.

Spotting them is hard. Detection tools look for odd details, such as skin colour or blinking, but they often fail on real-world pictures. Each time detection gets better, the fakes tend to catch up. Another approach marks real photos when they are taken. A hidden watermark or signed data then shows where a picture came from.

**Why it matters to you:** a photo of you that is online can be turned into something you never did. And a convincing picture or voice is no longer proof on its own. Check where it came from before you believe it or share it.
